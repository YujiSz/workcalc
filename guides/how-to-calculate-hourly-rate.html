How to Calculate Your Freelance Hourly Rate (The Realistic Way)

Why the standard corporate salary conversion will cause undercharging, and how to build a resilient pricing formula.

The Fatal Flaw of the 2,000-Hour Rule

When professionals transition from traditional employment to freelancing, they commonly reach for the standard corporate rule of thumb: take your previous full-time salary (e.g. $80,000) and divide it by 2,000 working hours (40 hours/week × 50 weeks), yielding an apparent hourly rate of $40/hour.

In practice, charging $40/hour under this assumption quickly results in financial distress. In an office job, an employer pays for your computer hardware, software licenses, paid sick leave, 4 weeks of paid annual vacation, employer payroll taxes, healthcare contributions, and legal administration.

Core Rule: As an independent contractor, you do not just deliver client work; you are simultaneously the CEO, lead generation specialist, accountant, and IT technician of a micro-agency.

The Reality of Non-Billable Business Time

No independent specialist bills 40 hours every week. A full freelance week consists of two completely distinct categories of labor:

  1. Billable Client Hours: Direct execution, coding, copywriting, design production, client strategy sessions.
  2. Non-Billable Overhead Hours: Client discovery calls, preparing proposals, invoicing, chasing late payments, bookkeeping, hardware updates, marketing, and self-education.

For most full-time freelancers, realistic billable utilization hovers between 55% and 65% of their total working week (around 22 to 26 billable hours out of a standard 40-hour work week). If you divide your income targets by 40 hours instead of your actual billable capacity, you automatically create a 35% to 45% revenue deficit.

The 5-Step Formula for a Resilient Rate

To establish a sustainable hourly rate that funds both your personal life and your business runway, execute this sequence:

Step 1: Define Target Annual Net Take-Home

Establish the exact net income your personal household requires every month, multiplied by 12. If your family needs $5,000 per month net, your annual net take-home target is $60,000.

Step 2: Add Direct Annual Business Overhead

Compile every business-related expense you incur annually: cloud software (Figma, Adobe, hosting), coworking or home office utilities, accounting fees, banking fees, laptop replacement depreciation, and professional liability insurance. If this totals $600/month, add $7,200 annually.

Step 3: Gross Up for Self-Employment Taxes & Reserves

Unlike W2 or PAYE employees, independent operators pay direct self-employment taxes plus corporate income contributions. Additionally, you need a 5% to 10% rainy day emergency reserve. If your combined tax and reserve rate is 25%, calculate your required gross annual target as:

Gross Annual Target = (Net Income + Annual Overhead) / (1 - TaxRate)

For example: ($60,000 + $7,200) / (1 - 0.25) = $67,200 / 0.75 = $89,600 Gross Target.

Step 4: Deduct Paid Time Off & Holidays

A full calendar year has 52 weeks. If you want 4 weeks of vacation and plan for 2 weeks of national holidays or sick days, your working year is 46 weeks.

Step 5: Factor in Billable Capacity

At 40 total hours per week across 46 working weeks, you have 1,840 total working hours. Applying a realistic 60% billable ratio leaves you with 1,104 billable client hours per year.

Now divide your gross target by your billable capacity: $89,600 / 1,104 = $81.16/hour. Notice how what initially felt like a $40/hour job requires an $82/hour freelance rate to yield the exact same take-home lifestyle.
